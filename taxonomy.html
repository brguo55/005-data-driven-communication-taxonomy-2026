A Taxonomy of Issues in Healthcare Data-Driven Communication for Clinical Decision-Making

Risk scores, prediction models, electronic health record summaries, dashboards, and algorithmic decision support systems now accompany clinicians at or near the point of care. A model can perform well statistically and still fail at the point of use if its output is difficult to interpret, invites inappropriate trust, or does not connect clearly to a decision.

We developed a preliminary taxonomy of issues in healthcare data-driven communication through a structured synthesis of 48 sources. Each of its eight dimensions is linked to the clinical decision-making risk it poses and to a corresponding opportunity for intervention.

The gap

Detailed standards exist for the manuscript, not for the point of use

Reporting standards for prediction model research specify in detail what authors should report about model development and evaluation. They are designed primarily for communication from researchers to readers of the scientific literature.

Researchers → readers of the scientific literature

Reporting how a model was developed and evaluated

  • TRIPOD+AIData sources, predictors, performance assessment, calibration, and external validation
  • CONSORT-AITrials of artificial intelligence interventions
  • DECIDE-AIEarly-stage clinical evaluation
Mature, detailed guidance

Deployed tool → clinicians and patients

Communicating an output at the point of use

Reporting standards can be satisfied even when a deployed interface presents a clinician with an output whose timeframe, uncertainty, reliability, or action implications are difficult to interpret.

The taxonomy

Eight dimensions between a tool's output and a clinical decision

The synthesis produced eight dimensions describing distinct but potentially overlapping ways in which data-driven communication can break down between a tool's output and a clinical decision. Each dimension is organized around its own analytic question and is mapped to a clinical decision-making risk and an opportunity for intervention.

Dimension 1 of 8

Risk information

What estimate is being communicated?

2of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

The semantic content of the risk estimate itself, before considering how that estimate is displayed.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Risk Estimate Ambiguity

    • Unclear probability estimates
    • Confusion between absolute and relative risk
    • Unspecified risk timeframe

  • Risk Stratification Problems

    • Overly broad risk categories
    • Unclear threshold justification
    • Inconsistent definitions of low-, medium-, or high-risk groups

  • Clinical Meaning of Risk

    • Unclear explanation of what the risk estimate means for patients, clinicians, or clinical action

Clinical decision-making risk

Patients or clinicians may misunderstand the magnitude, timeframe, or clinical meaning of a risk estimate.

Potential intervention opportunity

Use standardized risk labels; report absolute risk alongside relative risk; specify timeframe, denominator, baseline population, and risk group definition.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The first dimension concerns the semantic content of the risk estimate itself, before considering how that estimate is displayed. Risk estimate ambiguity arises when a probability is reported without information needed to interpret it, such as whether the estimate is absolute or relative, the relevant denominator, or the time horizon. Risk stratification problems arise when categories are overly broad, when thresholds separating low-, medium-, and high-risk groups are not justified, or when category definitions are applied inconsistently. Underlying both is the clinical meaning of risk: an estimate can be technically correct yet still leave clinicians or patients uncertain about what event is being predicted, over what period, and what the estimate implies for a decision.

Dimension 2 of 8

Uncertainty communication

What is known about its uncertainty?

11of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

The epistemic status of an estimate: how much uncertainty surrounds it and how that uncertainty should be interpreted.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Uncertainty Omission

    • Missing confidence intervals
    • Missing prediction intervals
    • No discussion of model uncertainty or uncertainty sources

  • Uncertainty Interpretation

    • Unclear distinction between confidence, variability, prediction error, and model limitations

  • Overstated Certainty

    • Risk estimates presented as more precise or definitive than the underlying evidence supports

Clinical decision-making risk

Decision-makers may overtrust or undertrust predictions when confidence, variability, or model limitations are not clearly communicated.

Potential intervention opportunity

Display confidence intervals, prediction intervals, uncertainty ranges, or qualitative uncertainty cues; explain major sources of uncertainty in plain language.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The second dimension concerns the epistemic status of an estimate: how much uncertainty surrounds it and how that uncertainty should be interpreted. Uncertainty omission occurs when confidence or prediction intervals are absent or when relevant sources of model uncertainty are not communicated. Uncertainty interpretation problems arise when distinctions among sampling uncertainty, outcome variability, prediction error, and model limitations are blurred. At the opposite extreme, overstated certainty presents an estimate as more precise or definitive than the available evidence supports. The studies grouped here collectively emphasize that displaying a number without communicating its limits can invite users to infer a degree of certainty that the underlying evidence does not warrant.

Dimension 3 of 8

Presentation and format

How is it encoded?

15of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

How information is encoded and presented, distinct from the substantive content being communicated.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Numeric Presentation

    • Percentages that are difficult to interpret
    • Inconsistent use of fractions, natural frequencies, or absolute risks
    • Excessive numerical precision

  • Visual Presentation

    • Misleading color scales
    • Unclear icon arrays
    • Poorly labeled charts, risk ladders, or visual decision aids

  • Textual Explanation

    • Vague wording
    • Limited plain-language explanation
    • Unclear explanation of what the displayed information means

Clinical decision-making risk

Numeric, textual, or visual formats may make risk information difficult to interpret, especially for users with different levels of numeracy or health literacy.

Potential intervention opportunity

Use patient-centered visual aids, icon arrays, natural frequencies, plain-language explanations, and accessible visualization design principles.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The third dimension concerns how information is encoded and presented, distinct from the substantive content being communicated. Numeric presentation issues include percentages that are difficult to interpret, inconsistent use of fractions, natural frequencies, and absolute risks, and unnecessary numerical precision. Visual presentation issues include misleading color scales, unclear icon arrays, and poorly labeled charts or risk ladders. Textual explanation issues include vague wording and the absence of a plain-language account of what a display means. The breadth of work in this dimension reflects an established evidence base showing that presentation format can alter comprehension and, intentionally or unintentionally, influence decisions.

Dimension 4 of 8

Patient and clinician interpretation

How may a clinician or patient interpret it?

3of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

Shifts from the communication artifact to the person interpreting it.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Numeracy Barriers

    • Difficulty interpreting probabilities
    • Confusion between population-level and individual-level risk
    • Limited understanding of statistical information

  • Cognitive Burden

    • Too much information
    • Complex displays
    • Limited time for interpretation during clinical consultation

  • Trust and Perceived Credibility

    • Overtrust in model outputs
    • Distrust due to unclear data sources
    • Misinterpretation of automated recommendations

Clinical decision-making risk

Users may misinterpret probabilities, confuse population-level evidence with individual-level risk, or place inappropriate trust in automated outputs.

Potential intervention opportunity

Provide interpretation guidance, calibration explanations, decision examples, and prompts that encourage critical review rather than passive acceptance.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The fourth dimension shifts from the communication artifact to the person interpreting it. Numeracy barriers include difficulty interpreting probabilities and confusion between population-level and individual-level risk. Cognitive burden arises when information volume, display complexity, or time pressure exceeds what a user can process during a clinical encounter. Trust and perceived credibility capture both directions of miscalibrated reliance: overtrusting an automated output because it appears authoritative and underusing a potentially helpful output when its source, rationale, or limitations are unclear. This dimension therefore concerns not simply whether information is available, but how users are likely to understand and respond to it.

Dimension 5 of 8

Decision support

How does it connect to an action?

5of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

The transition from information to action.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Actionability Problems

    • No clear next step
    • Weak connection to treatment options
    • Limited guidance for clinical decision-making

  • Workflow Integration Issues

    • Poor timing of information delivery
    • Alert fatigue
    • Lack of fit with clinical encounter workflow

  • Recommendation Communication

    • Automated recommendations are not clearly linked to clinical choices, treatment options, or decision thresholds

Clinical decision-making risk

Data-driven information may fail to support action if it is not connected to clinical choices, treatment options, or workflow timing.

Potential intervention opportunity

Link risk information to actionable next steps; integrate recommendations into clinical workflow; reduce alert fatigue through prioritization and contextual triggering.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The fifth dimension concerns the transition from information to action. Actionability problems arise when an output provides no clear connection to a next step or to the treatment options available in the clinical context. Workflow integration problems include poorly timed delivery, alert fatigue, and failure to fit the sequence and time constraints of the encounter. Recommendation communication problems arise when an automated recommendation is not clearly linked to the clinical choices, treatment options, or decision thresholds it is intended to inform. The defining question for this dimension is whether the communicated output can be incorporated into an actual clinical decision without creating additional ambiguity or workflow burden.

6

Dimension 6 of 8

Explainability and model communication

What does it communicate about model behavior and reliability?

5of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

Communication about the model itself (its behavior, performance, and appropriate conditions of use).

Issue types and subtypes

  • Explanation Misinterpretation

    • Feature importance mistaken as causality
    • Simplified explanations that hide uncertainty
    • Unclear distinction between association and causal explanation

  • Model Performance Communication

    • Calibration not explained
    • Accuracy metrics difficult to translate into clinical meaning
    • Unclear communication of false positives and false negatives

  • Conditions for Appropriate Use

    • Unclear communication of when model outputs should be used, reviewed cautiously, or overridden by clinical judgment

Clinical decision-making risk

Explanations may be mistaken for causal evidence, and model performance may be difficult to translate into clinical reliability.

Potential intervention opportunity

Use reliability-aware explanations; distinguish association from causation; communicate calibration, subgroup performance, false positives, false negatives, and appropriate use conditions.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The sixth dimension concerns communication about the model itself (its behavior, performance, and appropriate conditions of use). Explanation misinterpretation occurs when feature importance is read as causation or when a simplified explanation obscures uncertainty and model limitations. Model performance communication problems arise when calibration is not explained, when performance metrics are not translated into clinically meaningful consequences, or when false positive and false negative behavior is left unclear. Conditions for appropriate use capture the absence of guidance about when an output should be relied on, interpreted cautiously, or overridden by clinical judgment. This dimension is deliberately broader than explainability: knowing why a model produced an output is not equivalent to knowing whether that output is reliable in the present setting.

7

Dimension 7 of 8

Equity and subgroup reliability

Does its applicability differ across populations?

1of 47 coded source as primary dimension

Focus

Whether model reliability is communicated for the populations the tool is intended to serve.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Subgroup Performance Differences

    • Model performance may vary across patient subgroups, but subgroup-specific reliability is not clearly communicated

  • Data Representativeness

    • Missing data, biased samples, or underrepresentation of certain populations may limit the reliability of model outputs

  • Equity-Sensitive Interpretation

    • Users may not be informed when predictions should be interpreted cautiously for underrepresented or clinically distinct groups

Clinical decision-making risk

Model outputs may be less reliable for specific populations if subgroup performance, missing data, or representativeness limitations are not communicated.

Potential intervention opportunity

Report subgroup validation where available; flag data limitations; communicate when model outputs should be interpreted cautiously for underrepresented groups.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The seventh dimension concerns whether model reliability is communicated for the populations the tool is intended to serve. Subgroup performance differences arise when a model performs differently across patient groups but that variation is not surfaced to users. Data representativeness issues arise when missing data, biased samples, or underrepresentation of particular populations limit applicability without being made visible. Equity-sensitive interpretation concerns whether users are alerted when a prediction warrants additional caution for an underrepresented or clinically distinct group. Only one coded study primarily informed this dimension, so the category should be treated as an important analytic signpost rather than a mature evidence base within the present corpus.

8

Dimension 8 of 8

Shared decision-making

Does the resulting decision incorporate the patient's values?

5of 47 coded sources as primary dimension

Focus

Whether communication supports a decision that reflects the patient's values and preferences.

Issue types and subtypes

  • Patient Preference Integration

    • Patient preferences, goals, and values are not incorporated into the presentation or interpretation of risk information

  • Trade-off Communication

    • Benefits, harms, uncertainty, and treatment trade-offs are not clearly communicated to support shared decision-making

  • Clinician-Patient Discussion Support

    • Decision aids lack prompts or structures that support meaningful discussion between clinicians and patients

Clinical decision-making risk

Risk or uncertainty information may be presented in ways that do not support patient preferences, values, or trade-off discussions.

Potential intervention opportunity

Design decision aids that incorporate patient goals, preference-sensitive trade-offs, and clinician-patient discussion prompts.

These mappings are intended as design hypotheses rather than validated remedies.

Full description from the paper

The eighth dimension concerns whether communication supports a decision that reflects the patient's values and preferences. Patient preference integration problems arise when goals and values are absent from how information is presented or interpreted. Trade-off communication problems arise when benefits, harms, uncertainty, and treatment trade-offs are not made sufficiently clear for a preference-sensitive choice. Clinician-patient discussion support concerns whether tools and decision aids provide prompts or structure that help clinicians and patients deliberate together. The focus is therefore not merely on understanding an output, but on whether communication enables that output to contribute appropriately to a shared decision.

Dimensions are analytic lenses, not mutually exclusive bins. An inadequately calibrated risk score displayed with a misleading color scale and no indication of uncertainty may simultaneously involve presentation and format, uncertainty communication, and explainability and model communication.

Across dimensions

Three cross-cutting patterns

  • 2 · Uncertainty communication

Uncertainty is a design problem

Uncertainty communication is a design problem rather than a disclosure problem alone. An interval or quality of evidence label is useful only to the extent that users can interpret its meaning and incorporate it into a decision.

  • 6 · Explainability and model communication

Explanation and reliability are distinct targets

Model explanation and model reliability are related but distinct communication targets. Feature attribution displays and summary accuracy metrics may improve transparency while providing little information about calibration, transportability, or subgroup performance.

  • 1 · Risk information
  • 6 · Explainability and model communication
  • 8 · Shared decision-making

Several dimensions meet at the decision threshold

Choosing the threshold that converts a probability into an action entails judgments about the relative consequences of false positives and false negatives, linking statistical performance to clinical context and patient values. The same underlying choice can appear as a risk information problem, a model communication problem, or a shared decision-making problem, depending on where along the pathway it becomes visible.

Illustrative application

Three delirium prediction models, read through the taxonomy

To examine whether the dimensions can be recognized in concrete prediction model reports, we applied the taxonomy informally to three delirium prediction studies listed in the supplementary material of a recent scoping review of machine learning for delirium prediction using electronic health records. The exercise was intended to demonstrate interpretability of the taxonomy, not to estimate the frequency of communication problems in delirium prediction.

Abstract

Healthcare decision-making increasingly relies on data-driven tools, including risk prediction models, clinical decision support systems, dashboards, and algorithmic recommendations. The clinical value of these tools depends not only on model performance but also on whether their outputs are communicated in ways that clinicians and patients can interpret, appropriately trust, and use. Reporting standards such as TRIPOD+AI specify what model developers should report to readers of the scientific literature, but they do not provide an equivalent framework for what a deployed model should communicate at the point of use. We developed a preliminary taxonomy of issues in healthcare data-driven communication through a structured synthesis of 48 sources identified by PubMed search and citation searching. The taxonomy comprises eight dimensions: risk information; uncertainty communication; presentation and format; patient and clinician interpretation; decision support; explainability and model communication; equity and subgroup reliability; and shared decision-making. Each dimension is linked to the clinical decision-making risk it poses and to a corresponding opportunity for intervention. Coverage in the coded corpus was uneven, with equity and subgroup reliability informed by a single study. An illustrative application to three published delirium prediction studies shows how the taxonomy can distinguish communication practices in concrete prediction model settings. These findings identify clinician- and patient-facing communication as an important and comparatively underspecified component of prediction model deployment.

Methods

How the taxonomy was built

We developed the taxonomy through a structured synthesis of the literature using a directed content analysis approach. An initial organizing structure, informed by prior work, was applied and iteratively revised as additional themes emerged. The objective was conceptual coverage of ways in which data-driven communication can break down in clinical decision-making, rather than exhaustive retrieval or estimation of the prevalence of individual issue types.

PubMed search across three concept blocks

  • Communication of risk, probability, and uncertainty
  • AND
  • Healthcare and clinical settings, including the electronic health record
  • AND
  • Artificial intelligence, prediction models, and clinical decision support

Terms were combined within blocks and intersected across them. Animal-only studies were removed with the filter animals[mh] NOT humans[mh].

Purposive selection and citation searching

  1. 128Records screened by title and abstract
  2. 124Judged topically relevant (4 excluded)
  3. 10Seed papers advanced to focused full-text review (114 not advanced)
  4. 5Seeds used for backward and forward citation searching
  5. +38Additional sources retained after abstract review
  6. 48Sources in the final corpus: 47 coded articles and 1 book retained for context

One coded paper also served as a conceptual anchor for the initial category structure.

Common extraction and iterative synthesis

Each coded source was characterized with a common extraction framework covering clinical and decision context, intended audience, the information communicated, communication format, uncertainty and reliability, human interpretation and decision implications, and an emerging taxonomy theme.

Categories were merged, split, relabeled, and promoted to higher-level dimensions as the coded evidence suggested clearer boundaries. All screening, extraction, and initial thematic coding were performed by a single author (B.G.), and the category structure was reviewed and revised in discussion with the co-authors.

Limitations

  • A directed, scoping synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review. The database search was limited to PubMed, which likely underrepresents studies indexed primarily in computing, visualization, and human factors venues.
  • Purposive selection. Only 10 of the 124 records judged topically relevant were advanced as seed papers. Uneven coverage across dimensions is not evidence that some problems are more common or important than others.
  • A single coder. Individual records were not independently coded, and no inter-rater agreement statistics were computed.
  • A descriptive framework. The risk and intervention mappings in Table 4 have not been empirically validated.
